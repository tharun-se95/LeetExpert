## Caption

A hash table with a million slots collides after about a thousand inserts, not half a million.

Even with a perfectly uniform hash, two keys land in the same slot far sooner than intuition suggests.

It's the birthday paradox. With 365 slots, one per birthday, 23 people already give a 50.7% chance that two share one.

Scale it up: 1,000,000 slots, and after just 1,000 inserts there's a 39.3% chance of a collision. Better than even comes at 1,178 inserts, nowhere near the 500,000 you'd guess.

So a collision isn't your hash function making a mistake. It's arithmetic about slots and keys: a certainty to manage, not a defect to eliminate.

Did you expect it to be that early? 👇

## Alt text (per slide)

**Slide 1.** Cover. Hash Tables. A million-slot hash table collides after about a thousand inserts. Not half a million.

**Slide 2.** The intuition. Surely not until it is half full? A table with 1,000,000 slots and a perfectly uniform hash. You might assume two keys won't share a slot until around 500,000 inserts.

**Slide 3.** The birthday version. 365 slots, one per birthday People needed for a better-than-even chance of a shared slot: 23. The chance at 23 people: 50.7%.

**Slide 4.** The math. Every new key must miss every taken slot Python code: def collision_chance(slots, inserts): / clear = 1.0 / for i in range(inserts): / clear *= (slots - i) / slots / return 1 - clear. Those chances multiply, and the product shrinks fast.

**Slide 5.** Scale it. 1,000,000 slots Chance of a collision after 1,000 inserts: 39.3%. Inserts for a better-than-even chance: 1,178. About a thousand, not half a million.

**Slide 6.** So what. A guarantee, not a bug A collision isn't the hash function making a mistake. It is simple arithmetic about slots and keys. Collisions aren't a defect to eliminate. They are a certainty to *manage*.
